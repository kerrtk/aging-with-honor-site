For three consecutive competition cycles, a cluster of elite marathoners has followed an unusual pattern: skipping marquee international meets in favor of lower-profile regional races that fall outside the testing windows used by the International Federation of Endurance Sports (IFES).

Game Integrity Journal's review of publicly available competition calendars, combined with interviews with two former IFES testing coordinators, found that regional meets are inspected by independent doping-control officers roughly one-third as often as IFES-sanctioned majors — a gap athletes and agents describe, off the record, as an open secret.

## How the gap works

IFES's testing protocol prioritizes "high-likelihood" events: championships, Diamond-tier meets, and any race with prize money above a set threshold. Everything else is nominally subject to the same rules but, in practice, is tested at the discretion of national federations — many of which lack the budget for short-notice, out-of-competition collection.

<Callout label="How out-of-competition testing works">
  Athletes in a registered testing pool must submit quarterly whereabouts filings so doping-control officers can arrive unannounced. Enforcement depends entirely on whether a national federation has officers and budget to act on that information — which varies enormously between countries.
</Callout>

"You could see it in the entry lists," said one former coordinator, who asked not to be named because they still work in the sport. "Names would show up at a regional meet in a country with maybe two active testing officers, then disappear from anything IFES had eyes on for six weeks."

## A pattern, not a single case

Game Integrity Journal is not alleging that any specific athlete has violated anti-doping rules. What the calendar data shows is a structural incentive: an athlete who wants to minimize testing exposure without missing competition entirely has a well-worn path to do so, and it does not require breaking any written rule.

> The rules were written for a world where 'competing' meant showing up at the events everyone was watching. Nobody wrote a rule for the regional circuit because nobody thought it mattered enough to game.

IFES's anti-doping director, reached for comment, acknowledged the disparity in testing density and said a working group has been reviewing "risk-weighted" testing allocation that would follow athletes rather than events. No timeline for implementation has been announced.

## What happens next

Two national federations contacted for this story said they had not been asked to increase testing budgets for regional meets, despite the pattern identified here. Absent a change in how testing dollars are allocated, the gap Game Integrity Journal identified is likely to persist through at least the next competition cycle.

We will continue tracking IFES's working group and will update this story as the review progresses.
