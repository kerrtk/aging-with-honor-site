Player-prop bets — wagers on an individual athlete's statistics rather than a game's outcome — have become the fastest-growing product in the legal sports-betting market. Game Integrity Journal's review of licensing agreements between three major leagues and Meridian Sportsbook Group found that leagues are paid directly based on the volume of data consumed for these markets, creating a financial incentive to expand exactly the betting products integrity officers say carry the highest fixing and harassment risk.

## A financial incentive integrity offices don't control

League data-licensing revenue and league integrity-monitoring budgets are typically negotiated by entirely separate departments, according to two league officials who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to discuss internal budgeting. That means the office responsible for expanding a lucrative data product and the office responsible for monitoring the risk that product creates rarely sit in the same room.

<Callout label="Why player props carry more risk">
  A player prop only requires influencing one individual's statistical output — missed shots, a single at-bat, a specific quarter — rather than an entire team's result, which integrity analysts say makes these markets structurally easier to manipulate and harder to detect.
</Callout>

"Match-fixing detection systems were built around final scores and point spreads," said an integrity-monitoring consultant who has worked with two of the leagues named in this story. "Retrofitting them to catch anomalous betting patterns on a single player's third-quarter rebound total is a much harder technical problem, and the money to solve it hasn't scaled with the money coming in from the data deals themselves."

## Athletes say they weren't consulted

Player associations for two of the three leagues confirmed to Game Integrity Journal that athletes were not formally consulted before data-licensing deals covering prop-bet markets were signed, despite those markets directly exposing individual players to public scrutiny, harassment, and — in a small number of publicized cases — direct contact from bettors seeking to influence performance.

> Nobody asked us if we wanted our missed free throws turned into a betting product. We found out the same way the fans did.

## What leagues say they're doing

All three leagues pointed Game Integrity Journal to existing integrity-monitoring partnerships and said additional funding for anomaly detection is under review. None committed to a specific budget increase tied to prop-bet market growth, and none said athlete consultation would become a formal part of future data-licensing negotiations.
