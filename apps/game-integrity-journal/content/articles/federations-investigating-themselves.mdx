There is a pattern that repeats across nearly every governance scandal in sport, regardless of the federation, the country, or the specific misconduct alleged: the organization under scrutiny appoints the panel that investigates it, funds that panel, and retains the right to decide which findings become public.

## The independence illusion

Federations describe these arrangements as "independent reviews," and the panels are often staffed by credible, well-regarded professionals — retired judges, respected administrators, experienced investigators. But independence of the individuals on a panel is not the same as independence of the process. A reviewer who is selected by, paid by, and reporting to the organization being reviewed operates inside a structure with an obvious, if unspoken, incentive: findings that are too damaging risk the reviewer's future engagements.

<Callout label="What real independence would require">
  Genuinely independent oversight typically requires three things missing from most federation-led reviews: a selecting body separate from the accused organization, a budget the organization cannot revoke mid-investigation, and a binding requirement to publish findings in full.
</Callout>

I don't think this is usually a matter of bad faith on the part of the professionals involved. I think it's a structural problem that produces predictable results regardless of who sits on the panel: reviews that are thorough on process and vague on consequence, that name "systemic failures" without naming the people responsible for them, and that recommend reforms with no enforcement mechanism attached.

> A federation grading its own homework will always find room for partial credit.

## What would actually change this

A handful of national anti-doping agencies and one international federation's ethics office have experimented with a different model: statutory independence, where the reviewing body is established by law or treaty rather than by the organization it oversees, with its own budget and publication authority. These models are not perfect, but they remove the single biggest structural conflict — the reviewed party's ability to shape what gets reviewed and what gets released.

Until more federations adopt something like that model, "independent review" will keep meaning exactly what it has meant for the past decade: an investigation the accused organization gets to design.
