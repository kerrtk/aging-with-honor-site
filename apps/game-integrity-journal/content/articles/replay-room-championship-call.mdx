From the stands, a replay review looks like deliberation: officials huddled under a hood, fans chanting, a decision rendered after an agonizing wait. Inside the centralized replay operations center that handles reviews for the Continental Basketball Officials Association, the reality is faster, more procedural, and shaped by constraints most viewers never see.

Game Integrity Journal was granted rare access to observe a regular-season replay center shift on the condition that specific game outcomes discussed here not be reported until after the season concludes.

## The two-minute problem

CBOA's internal guidelines target a two-minute ceiling for most replay decisions, a benchmark set after broadcast partners raised concerns about game pacing. Replay officials described that ceiling as the single biggest factor shaping how reviews actually run.

<Callout label="What gets reviewed — and what doesn't">
  Replay is generally limited to specific reviewable categories written into league rulebooks: shot-clock violations, out-of-bounds calls, and flagrant-foul upgrades, for example. Judgment calls like block-versus-charge are explicitly excluded from most systems, even when video appears to show a clear error.
</Callout>

"You have camera angles loading, you're talking to the crew chief, and there's a clock running in your head the whole time," one replay official said. "Two minutes sounds like plenty until you're the one making the call with forty thousand people staring at a scoreboard."

> Fans think we're debating. Most of the time we're triaging — figuring out which angle actually answers the question fast enough to matter.

## Where the system breaks down

The center's own data, shared with Game Integrity Journal, shows that reviews taking longer than the two-minute target are more than twice as likely to be overturned on appeal to the league office — a pattern officials interpret as evidence that rushed reviews sometimes get it wrong, not that longer reviews are indecisive.

CBOA said it is piloting an extended-review category for postseason games only, allowing up to four minutes for close calls with championship implications. The pilot has not been made public until now.

## The transparency gap

Perhaps the most striking finding: none of the reasoning behind a replay decision is routinely published. Fans see a call reversed or upheld, with no access to which camera angle proved decisive or what evidentiary standard was applied.

CBOA said it is "evaluating options" for post-game explanation reports but has not committed to a timeline. Until it does, the replay room remains one of the least transparent rooms in professional sport — even as the decisions made inside it increasingly decide outcomes.
