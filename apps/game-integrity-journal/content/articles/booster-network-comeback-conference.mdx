A loosely organized network of boosters tied to three Comeback Conference athletic programs has been coordinating name, image, and likeness offers to recruits well outside the visibility of each school's compliance office, according to financial disclosures reviewed by Game Integrity Journal and interviews with four former athletics staff members.

## What the records show

NIL collectives — donor-funded entities that pool money to pay athletes for endorsements — are legal and, since 2021, a normal part of college recruiting. What Game Integrity Journal found unusual was coordination *between* collectives at nominally competing schools, apparently intended to steer recruits toward whichever program had a temporary need, in exchange for reciprocal steering later.

<Callout label="What compliance offices are supposed to catch">
  NCAA rules still prohibit NIL deals functioning as pay-for-play inducements tied to enrollment decisions, and require schools to maintain some oversight of collective activity. Compliance offices are typically responsible for flagging deals that look like disguised recruiting inducements.
</Callout>

"We had two people covering NIL disclosures for fourteen sports," said one former compliance staffer at a Comeback Conference school, speaking on condition of anonymity because of an ongoing consulting relationship with the conference. "We were reading contracts after the fact, not reviewing them before money moved. There was no way we'd have caught coordination between schools even if we'd known to look for it."

## Three programs, one pattern

Financial disclosures filed by the collectives themselves — required under state NIL transparency laws in two of the three states involved — show unusually similar payment timing to recruits who ultimately signed with a different program than the one whose collective had made the earlier offer, consistent with recruits being "passed along" once a roster need was filled elsewhere.

> None of this shows up in a compliance file, because none of it is technically a violation of any single school's rules. It's a violation of the spirit of the whole system, and there's no compliance office built to catch that.

Game Integrity Journal reached out to all three athletic departments named in the financial disclosures. Two declined to comment on specific collective activity, citing ongoing conference review; one confirmed it had requested more disclosure from its affiliated collective following inquiries for this story.

## What the conference says

A Comeback Conference spokesperson said the conference "takes NIL compliance seriously" and pointed to a new model policy under discussion that would require collectives to disclose cross-institutional communication. No enforcement mechanism for the policy has yet been proposed.

Game Integrity Journal will continue to follow the conference's review.
