Automated ball-strike systems are being sold on a simple promise: take the judgment call away from a human, and the controversy disappears. Having spent years around officiating crews and the technology meant to support them, I think that promise is only half true — and the half that's wrong could do real damage.

## Consistency is not the same as legitimacy

An automated system can call the same pitch the same way every time. That is a genuine, valuable improvement over human strike zones, which drift over the course of a game and vary between umpires. But consistency answers a narrower question than the one fans actually care about, which is: *can I trust the person or system making this call, and is there someone accountable if it's wrong?*

<Callout label="Where automation still needs a human">
  Every automated strike-zone system deployed so far keeps a human umpire behind the plate to handle check-swings, catcher's interference, and — critically — to serve as the visible, accountable authority fans and players can address in the moment.
</Callout>

A camera system has no face. It cannot be argued with, cannot explain its reasoning in the moment, and — when it fails, as any sensor system eventually will — there is no clear chain of accountability for players or fans to follow. We have already seen minor league games where a tracking system's calibration drifted mid-game and no one in the stadium had the authority or information to intervene until the half-inning ended.

> Replacing judgment with automation doesn't eliminate the need for accountability. It just moves accountability somewhere fans can't see it.

## The real fix is boring

The unglamorous truth is that most of the credibility problem in umpiring isn't really about accuracy — it's about transparency and consequence. Leagues that publish umpire accuracy grades, explain close calls publicly, and apply real consequences for repeated errors see far less controversy than leagues relying on secrecy, automated or not.

Automated systems can be a genuine part of the answer, particularly for objective, rule-bound calls like ball-strike or line calls. But leagues adopting them should be honest that they are solving a precision problem, not a trust problem — and pair the technology with the transparency measures that actually rebuild trust.
